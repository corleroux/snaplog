Meta Global AI Developer Hackathon Participant Guide
Welcome to the Meta Global AI Developer Hackathon (the “Hackathon”)! We’re thrilled to have you on board.

This guide is your all-in-one resource for the event, including schedule, rules, technical resources, problem statements, judging information, and more. Please read this carefully; most answers can be found here.

1. Your Goal: Hackathon Problem Statements
Your project must build towards one of the following problem statements:

Build for Your World - Bring Meta's models into your domain. Solve a problem you know firsthand, free up time and mental space, and make something possible that wasn't before.

Push the Frontier - Use Meta's models to tackle challenges in software, robotics, manufacturing, energy, materials and beyond. Give a machine a new capability, accelerate a scientific experiment, or unlock a better way to understand and control a physical system. Show us what becomes possible when these models meet the real world.

Your project must incorporate Meta's models. Use the Muse Spark series, Muse Wonder, Muse Image Muse Voice Transcribe and SAM 3.1 through Meta Model API, run Meta open weights models Muse Glimmer and Muse Spark Open, and code faster with Muse Code coding agent.

4. Hackathon Rules
Open source: Repositories must be public.

Team size: A maximum of four participants per team. Solo participants are allowed.

Demo requirements: Your demo must only highlight the specific features, code, and functionality that you built during the Hackathon. Judges must be able to clearly identify what was created during the Hackathon. Failure to clearly identify your original contributions will result in immediate disqualification at Judges sole discretion.

New work only: What you present at this Hackathon must have been built during the event. This should be clear in your submissions. Startups may build on existing products or codebases, but you must clearly identify what was newly developed during the Hackathon.

Banned projects: Projects will be disqualified if they: violate legal, ethical, or platform policies, use code, data, or assets you do not have the rights to.

8. Judging Process
Judging Process will happen in three stages:

Stage One: The first stage will determine via pass/fail whether the ideas meet a baseline level of viability, in that the Project reasonably fits the theme and reasonably used Meta Model API and/or Meta's open-weight models to build the project in the Hackathon.

Stage Two: All Submissions that pass Stage One will be evaluated in Stage Two based on the following criteria using a scale from 0-10:

Judging Rubric

Category (Weight)

Description

Impact (30%)

What's the real-world potential here? Who benefits, and how much does it matter? Could this actually become something people use? Does it fit into one of the problem statements listed above?

Use of Meta AI Tooling (25%)

How creatively did this team use Meta's AI tooling? Did they go beyond a basic integration? Did they surface capabilities that surprised even us?

Technical Depth & Execution (20%)

Is the engineering sound and thoughtfully refined? Does this feel like something that was thought through with real craft.

Demo (25%) 

Is this a working, impressive demo? Does the product hold up live? Is the demo video genuinely cool to watch?

Stage Three: The top 5 scoring projects per track move on to a final round of judging, where final round Judges will watch the video demos live and rank them using the same rubric.

Judging criteria:

The criteria are: Impact 30%, Use of Meta AI Tooling 25%, Technical Depth and Execution 20%, and Demo 25%. Complexity isn't scored for its own sake, so a focused prototype that solves a real problem, uses Meta's models well and has a clear working demo scores well.

Judging happens in three stages. Stage One is pass/fail: the project has to reasonably fit one of the two themes and reasonably use Meta's models. Stage Two scores the project from 0 to 10 on four criteria: Impact (30%), Use of Meta AI Tooling (25%), Technical Depth and Execution (20%) and Demo (25%). The top 5 per track then go to live final-round judging.

Judges want a focused project that solves a real problem with Meta's models, makes creative use of them beyond a basic integration, and has a working demo. Your 3-minute video should open with one line on what existed before and what you built this week, then show the working product rather than slides. Commit incrementally, since judges look at commit history.

Yes, open-source Meta models anyone can access, such as Llama, count as Meta models however you run them, including locally on your own compute, so one Llama model running locally for the core text-analysis feature is eligible. The rule is that every model your project calls at runtime must be a Meta model, and standard application code is not a model call. Synthetic demo inputs with real model inference are fine. In your README, say which open Meta model you use and where it runs.

For judging, Meta's models must be a real part of what the project runs, and Use of Meta AI Tooling (25%) rewards creative use beyond a basic integration; the criteria are Impact 30%, Use of Meta AI Tooling 25%, Technical Depth and Execution 20% and Demo 25%. Combining a model with deterministic code is fine as long as the model does real work in the flow.

The demo video is judged under two Official Rules criteria: Demo (25%) rewards a working, impressive demo whose product holds up live and is genuinely cool to watch, and Use of Meta AI Tooling (25%) rewards going beyond a basic integration. Technical Depth & Execution is another 20%, and Impact 30%. Show the product working rather than slides, open with one line on what was pre-existing and what you built this week, and keep it to 3:00 since judges stop watching at the 3-minute mark.

Stage Two rubric (same for both tracks): Impact 30%, Use of Meta AI Tooling 25%, Technical Depth & Execution 20%, Demo 25%. Stage One is a pass/fail check that the project fits a theme and uses Meta models.
